Group discussion: how to do well

A group discussion is not a debate to be won. Assessors are choosing people they would want in a meeting: those who think clearly, listen and help the group move forward.

What is being assessed

  • Content: do you have relevant points, facts and examples?
  • Communication: are you clear, brief and audible?
  • Listening: do you build on what others say?
  • Group behaviour: are you courteous? Do you let others speak, bring in quiet members, and keep the discussion on track?
  • Reasoning: can you see more than one side?

Speaking the most is not on the list. A candidate who makes three good points and one useful summary usually scores above one who talks for half the time.

Kinds of topic

  • Factual or current: "Online education: boon or bane", "Electric vehicles in India".
  • Abstract: "Blue is better than red", "Zero".
  • Case: a short situation with a decision to be made.

The first minute

You are usually given a minute or two to think. Use it to write:

  1. What the topic means, in one line.
  2. Three or four points, with an example for each. A quick way to find points is to look at the topic from different sides: economic, social, practical, for the individual, for the country, short term and long term.
  3. A likely point on the other side, and your answer to it.

Starting

Opening the discussion gets you noticed, but only if you have something clear to say. A good opening defines the topic and suggests how to discuss it; it does not give a conclusion. "Friends, the topic is whether online education is a boon or a bane. I suggest we look first at its advantages, then its problems, and then at what makes it work. To begin, one clear advantage is reach..." If you have nothing ready, let someone else begin and come in second or third with a solid point.

Entering and adding

Wait for a speaker to finish a sentence, then come in on a link:

  • "Adding to what Priya said, ..."
  • "That is a good point about cost. There is another side to it: ..."
  • "May I bring in an example here?"
  • "We have talked about students; what about teachers?"

Make one point at a time, in about thirty seconds, with a fact or example. Then stop. Three or four such entries across the discussion are enough.

Disagreeing politely

Disagree with the point, not the person, and never with heat.

  • "I see it a little differently, because ..."
  • "That may be true in cities. In villages, however, ..."
  • "I agree in part. Where I differ is ..."

Helping the group

These actions are noticed and valued:

  • Bringing in someone who has not spoken: "Rahul, you wanted to say something?"
  • Calming a noisy patch: "Friends, we are all speaking together. Shall we take it one at a time?"
  • Bringing the discussion back: "We have moved to exams in general; coming back to online classes..."
  • Summarising midway: "So far we have three advantages and two concerns."

Summarising at the end

If you get the chance, give a fair summary of the whole discussion in thirty seconds: the main points on each side and where the group largely agreed. Do not add new points or push only your own view.

What to avoid

  • Shouting, interrupting, pointing fingers or getting personal.
  • Speaking only to the assessors. Look at the group.
  • Long speeches.
  • Staying silent throughout. Even two good points are far better than none.
  • Making up statistics. If you are unsure of a figure, say "a large share" instead of inventing a number.
  • Switching sides repeatedly, or agreeing with everyone.
  • Slang, and mixing languages if the discussion is to be in English.

If you know nothing about the topic

Listen for the first two minutes; you will pick up the outline. Then enter by asking a sensible question, giving an example from daily life, or summarising what has been said. Honesty and good listening can carry you through.

How to prepare

  • Read a newspaper daily and note two points for and against on one issue.
  • Practise with five or six friends once a week: ten minutes of discussion, then five minutes of feedback to each other.
  • Practise speaking on any topic for one minute without stopping.
  • Record yourself to check speed and clarity.

Questions people ask

How many times should I speak in a group discussion?

Three or four clear contributions of about thirty seconds each, plus a summary if you get the chance, are enough.

Is it necessary to speak first?

No. Start only if you can define the topic and set a direction. A strong second or third entry is just as good.

What if others do not let me speak?

Wait for the end of a sentence and come in firmly with a link to the previous point. If the group is noisy, politely suggest taking turns; assessors notice who restores order.
