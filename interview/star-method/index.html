The STAR method for interview answers

When an interviewer says "tell me about a time when...", they want a story with a beginning, a middle and an end. STAR is the simplest way to tell one.

What STAR stands for

  • Situation: where and when. One or two sentences of background.
  • Task: what had to be done, or what the problem was, and your responsibility in it.
  • Action: what you did, step by step. This is the longest part.
  • Result: what happened in the end, with a number if possible, and what you learned.

When to use it

For behavioural questions, which ask about the past: "Tell me about a time you worked under pressure", "Describe a conflict with a colleague", "Give an example of a mistake you made", "When did you show leadership?". The reasoning is that how you actually behaved is a better guide than how you say you would behave. STAR keeps your answer specific and stops you from wandering.

A worked example

Question: Tell me about a time you took the initiative.

  • Situation: "In my internship at a CA firm, client bills were kept in loose bundles, and during audit the seniors lost a lot of time looking for papers."
  • Task: "Nobody had asked me to fix it, but I was the one sent to find the bills, so I felt it most."
  • Action: "I asked my senior for permission to reorganise one client's file as a trial. I sorted the bills by month, numbered them, and made an index sheet in Excel with bill number, date, party and amount. It took me three evenings. When he saw it worked, I did the same for the other five clients over two weeks."
  • Result: "A bill that used to take ten minutes to find could be found in under one. The firm kept the system after I left, and my senior mentioned it in my internship letter. I learned that a small improvement is easier to get accepted if you show it working first."

Spoken at a normal pace, that answer takes about a minute and a half.

Proportions

Most weak answers spend a minute on the situation and ten seconds on the action. Reverse it: about 15% situation, 10% task, 60% action, 15% result. The interviewer is hiring you for what you do, so the action is where you earn the marks.

Say "I"

In team stories, candidates say "we did this, we decided that", and the interviewer cannot tell what the candidate contributed. Give the team its due in one phrase, then say what you did yourself: "The team had four members. My part was the database. I..."

Build a story bank

You cannot invent good stories in the interview room. Prepare five or six beforehand, each in STAR form, and most behavioural questions can be answered by choosing one.

Story aboutAnswers questions on
A tight deadline you metPressure, time management, planning
A disagreement you resolvedConflict, teamwork, communication
A mistake you made and correctedFailure, honesty, learning
Something you improved without being askedInitiative, problem solving
A difficult customer or personPatience, service, persuasion
A time you led or organised othersLeadership, responsibility

Freshers can draw stories from college projects, internships, part-time work, events, sports, NSS or NCC, and family responsibilities. A real small story is better than an impressive invented one, because follow-up questions quickly expose an invention.

Results without numbers

If there is no figure, state a clear outcome: the project was submitted on time, the customer stayed, the teacher adopted your method, the error did not recur. Then add the lesson in one line.

Common faults

  • A general answer with no story: "I always work well under pressure."
  • A story with no result.
  • A story in which the problem was entirely someone else's fault.
  • Going on for five minutes. Aim for about two.
  • Using the same story for every question.

Practise aloud

Write each story as four short notes, not a script. Then say it aloud, time it, and trim. The practice tool on this site gives you questions and a two minute timer. For model answers to the commonest behavioural question, see tell me about a challenge.

Questions people ask

What does STAR stand for?

Situation, Task, Action, Result: a four-part structure for answering behavioural interview questions with a specific example.

How long should a STAR answer be?

About one and a half to two minutes, with most of the time on the action you took.

Can freshers use the STAR method?

Yes. Use examples from projects, internships, part-time work, college events or family responsibilities.
